\begin{abstract}
Broadcast communication is fundamental to resource-constrained devices (RCDs),
which must distribute data to many receivers while conserving energy and
bandwidth. Inf-TESLA++ recently addressed continuous authentication in this
setting by anchoring hash-chain commitment keys in an Ethereum smart contract,
which removes the need for direct sender--receiver resynchronization. A weakness
remains: the probabilistic mode of Inf-TESLA++ runs on a fixed slot duration.
When the disclosure pipeline is stressed faster than this fixed rate can drain
it, the sender's disclosure queue overflows and the corresponding evidence is
lost permanently, breaking authentication for those slots at every receiver.
This paper presents Prob-Adaptive Inf-TESLA++, a backward-compatible extension
that replaces the fixed slot timer with a closed-loop timer driven by a local
congestion signal. At each slot boundary the sender normalizes its pending
disclosure backlog to a target utilization and nudges the next slot duration
with a bounded proportional (EIP-1559-style) rule, clamped to immutable on-chain
bounds $[T_{\min},T_{\max}]$ stored through a new \texttt{storeAdaptiveKey}
function. Longer slots batch more messages per disclosure and lower the
disclosure rate, letting the radio drain the backlog; shorter slots restore low
latency once pressure clears. We implement the extension on the Inf-TESLA++ Go
codebase with a Solidity contract, and evaluate it end-to-end against a live
local blockchain by sweeping the authentication-channel radio budget---the
resource whose overflow actually destroys authentication. The measured
controller tracks the bottleneck: the slot duration rises from its $1000$\,ms
floor to its $8000$\,ms ceiling as the budget tightens, the disclosure backlog
stays bounded across the viable range, and authentication survives down to a
hard budget floor below which no slot length can save it. We report the
controller's characteristic curves, quantify the stability boundary, and give an
honest account of the prototype's limitations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Resource-constrained devices such as sensors, drones, IoT nodes, and vehicular
on-board units depend on broadcast communication to reach many receivers at once
while keeping energy and bandwidth costs low~\cite{inftesla}. Securing such
traffic involves three goals: source authentication, data integrity, and
continuous authentication. The TESLA family~\cite{tesla} handles the first two
through a timed, hash-chain key disclosure scheme. Continuous authentication,
that is, sustaining authentication across the expiry of a finite keychain
without an expensive resynchronization step, stayed unsolved until
Inf-TESLA++~\cite{inftesla}.

Inf-TESLA++ removes direct sender--receiver communication during
resynchronization by placing keychain commitment keys in an Ethereum smart
contract, so any receiver can recover the commitment through a blockchain query.
Its probabilistic mode maps a batch of packets into a Bloom filter~\cite{bloom}
and broadcasts a single HMAC over the filter, which lowers communication overhead
during busy periods. The choice between the deterministic and probabilistic
modes is governed by the congestion metric $C = R/L$, the fraction of empty slots
the sender observes, with the probabilistic mode engaged once $C \geq \eta$ for a
threshold $\eta$ published by the consortium manager~\cite{inftesla}.

\textbf{Motivation.} Inf-TESLA++ employs a fixed slot duration for both
deterministic and probabilistic modes. Under bursty traffic, hardware slowdown,
or adversarial flooding, disclosure events may accumulate faster than the radio
can transmit them, causing queue overflow and permanent key loss. In the
probabilistic mode the loss is especially damaging: the disclosed object contains
the Bloom filter itself, so if it is dropped the receiver cannot test slot
membership and \emph{every} message batched into that slot becomes
unauthenticatable. Increasing the Bloom-filter size is not a viable remedy,
because the false-positive rate bounds the number of keys per batch, and a
permanently long slot inflates authentication latency for all traffic. We
therefore adapt the slot duration dynamically, allowing the disclosure queue to
drain during congestion while restoring the minimum slot duration once conditions
recover.

\textbf{Contributions.} This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
\item A closed-loop adaptive slot timer driven by a local congestion signal,
using a bounded proportional (EIP-1559-style) update rather than an unbounded
doubling/halving step, so the slot converges toward a target queue utilization
without overshoot.
\item A backward-compatible smart-contract extension that stores immutable timing
bounds $[T_{\min},T_{\max}]$ and returns them in a single query, preserving the
TESLA timing condition and blocking timing-manipulation denial of service.
\item An end-to-end evaluation that runs the full stack---local blockchain,
consortium manager, key owner, and RCD---and sweeps the authentication-channel
radio budget, demonstrating that the slot duration tracks the bottleneck, the
disclosure backlog stays bounded, and authentication survives to an identifiable
budget floor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{The TESLA Protocol Family}
TESLA~\cite{tesla} authenticates broadcast packets using delayed key disclosure
over a one-way hash chain. A sender commits to a reversed chain
$K_i = H(K_{i+1})$, so $K_0$ is a public commitment and any $K_i$ can be verified
by re-hashing to $K_0$. In slot $i$ the sender MACs its messages with the still
secret $K_i$ and reveals $K_i$ only after a delay of $d$ slots. Receivers buffer
packets until the key is disclosed, then check that it belongs to the committed
chain and validates the MAC; a forger who waits for $K_i$ has already missed the
window in which injection would be useful. $\mu$TESLA~\cite{spins} adapts TESLA to
resource-constrained sensor networks but requires periodic resynchronization and
scales poorly. TESLA++~\cite{teslapp} reduces receiver buffering by disclosing the
message and key together.

\subsection{Continuous Authentication in TESLA Variants}
Prior work has addressed continuous authentication using dual hash
chains~\cite{inftesla_orig}, encrypted next-chain commitments~\cite{enhanced},
hierarchical key structures~\cite{empirical,comparative}, and
blockchain-assisted commitment storage~\cite{garcia}. Inf-TESLA++~\cite{inftesla}
anchors infinite hash-chain commitment keys in a smart contract, eliminating
sender-assisted resynchronization, and serves as the baseline for this work.
However, none of these approaches adapts the disclosure schedule to prevent
sender-side disclosure-queue overflow under congestion.

\subsection{Congestion Control in Broadcast Authentication}
Conventional congestion-control mechanisms such as AIMD~\cite{aimd} require
per-receiver feedback and are therefore unsuitable for authenticated broadcast,
where a one-way, time-triggered sender receives no acknowledgements. Erasure
coding~\cite{rabin}, retransmission~\cite{spins}, and adaptive
batching~\cite{inftesla} improve reliability but do not address sender-side
disclosure-queue overflow. Our controller instead closes the loop on a purely
local signal---the sender's own disclosure backlog---and borrows the bounded
proportional update of the EIP-1559 fee mechanism~\cite{eip1559}, which likewise
steers a quantity toward a target utilization with a clamped multiplicative step.

\section{System and Threat Model}

\subsection{System Architecture}
Prob-Adaptive Inf-TESLA++ keeps the entity model of Inf-TESLA++~\cite{inftesla}
intact while extending the smart-contract interface and the sender's slot timer.

The \emph{transmitting RCD} ($r_s$) is a device with limited CPU, memory, power,
and bandwidth. It holds an infinite hash chain anchored in the contract and runs
in deterministic or probabilistic mode as before. The extension adds one duty: at
the close of each slot cycle $i$, $r_s$ samples its local disclosure-queue
occupancy, computes the congestion signal, and re-arms the timer with the next
duration $T_{\mathrm{next}}$ before running the next slot. The slot work
itself---the Bloom-filter flush and HMAC broadcast---is unchanged.

The \emph{receiving RCD} ($r_e$) buffers incoming packets and queries
\texttt{getKey} for the commitment key $K_0$, as in the baseline. It carries one
extra obligation: it must not accept a disclosed key until at least $T_{\max}$,
read from the contract, has elapsed since the packet was sent, irrespective of
the interval $r_s$ currently uses. This conservative wait preserves the timing
condition without forcing $r_e$ to track the sender's live slot duration.

The \emph{smart contract} retains every Inf-TESLA++ function (\texttt{init},
\texttt{regOwner}, \texttt{storeKey}, \texttt{setKey}, \texttt{getKey}) and adds
\texttt{storeAdaptiveKey} (Section~\ref{sec:contract}), which records $T_{\min}$
and $T_{\max}$ with the commitment key. The \emph{transport} is a connectionless
UDP channel with variable loss and stochastic latency, typical of IoT, LPWAN, and
VANET links. The device shares a single radio between application data and
authentication traffic; we treat the authentication path (HMACs and disclosures)
as the scarce resource, because it is the path whose overflow destroys
authentication.

\subsection{Threat Model}
We use the Dolev--Yao model~\cite{dolev} and add threats unique to dynamic timing.

The \emph{Dolev--Yao attacker} controls the channel and may intercept, delay,
inject, and replay packets. It is computationally bounded: it cannot invert $H$,
cannot forge $\mathrm{HMAC}_{K_i}$ without the undisclosed $K_i$, and cannot read
future keys, since $K_i = H(K_{i+1})$ is one-way.

A \emph{desynchronization attack} is unique to the adaptive setting. The
adversary injects, replays, or delays packets carrying manipulated sequence
numbers or timing information to make a receiver infer a slot duration different
from the one actually used by $r_s$. The goal is to induce premature acceptance
of disclosed keys, violating the TESLA timing condition, or to force receivers
into excessive waiting, resulting in liveness degradation.

For \emph{resource exhaustion and denial-of-service (DoS)}, we consider an
adversary that attempts to exhaust the sender's disclosure resources, either by
injecting sustained traffic to force the disclosure queue toward overflow, or by
maintaining persistent congestion so that the adaptive controller remains at its
maximum slot duration $T_{\max}$, degrading authentication throughput.

\section{The Prob-Adaptive Protocol Design}

\subsection{Overview}
In the baseline, $r_s$ fires a fixed \texttt{time.Ticker} every $T$\,ms, runs the
slot, and repeats. Prob-Adaptive Inf-TESLA++ swaps the ticker for a dynamic timer
re-armed with $T_{\mathrm{next}}$ after each slot, where $T_{\mathrm{next}}$
follows from the congestion signal measured over that slot. Everything
else---hash-chain key selection, Bloom-filter construction, HMAC computation and
broadcast, receiver verification, and \texttt{getKey} access---stays the same.
The extension is purely additive.

\subsection{Smart Contract Extension}\label{sec:contract}
The new function extends \texttt{storeKey} with two parameters:
\begin{equation}
\texttt{storeAdaptiveKey}(r_s, d, l, \tau_{st}, K_0, T_{\min}, T_{\max}).
\end{equation}
It performs the same signature check as \texttt{storeKey}, then stores
$(T_{\min},T_{\max})$ next to $(d,l,\tau_{st},K_0)$. Both bounds are given in
milliseconds and are immutable for the session. \texttt{getKey} is widened to
return $(K_0,d,l,\tau_{st},T_{\min},T_{\max})$, so a receiver obtains the entire
timing policy in one query.

Because $T_{\max}$ lives on-chain, a compromised sender can neither inflate the
slot without limit to stall authentication nor shrink it below $T_{\min}$ to
force premature acceptance. Any receiver can flag a violation by comparing the
observed slot duration against the on-chain record, inheriting the
tamper-evidence the baseline already relies on.

\subsection{Congestion Signal}\label{sec:metrics}
At each slot boundary the sender reads bounded local quantities that need no
network round-trip. The primary signal is the disclosure-queue pressure
\begin{equation}
D_i = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{len}}}{Q_{\mathrm{cap}}},
\label{eq:di}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\mathrm{len}}$ is the number of \emph{pending disclosure objects}---the
$(K_i,b_i)$ bundles produced but not yet transmitted, counting both the $d$
in-flight objects held by the TESLA delay and any that are queued behind a busy
radio---and $Q_{\mathrm{cap}}$ is the small capacity the device budgets for them.
A full queue is precisely what causes the irrecoverable key loss the static
protocol cannot avoid, so $D_i$ is the signal the controller must act on.

The design also admits a secondary, latency-based term
\begin{equation}
B_i = \min\!\left(1.0,\ L_{\mathrm{avg}}/L_{\mathrm{base}}\right),
\label{eq:bi}
\end{equation}
with $L_{\mathrm{avg}}$ the mean broadcast latency over the current window and
$L_{\mathrm{base}}$ a healthy per-device baseline; the clamp keeps
$B_i\in[0,1]$. The general congestion score is a convex combination
\begin{equation}
C_i = W_{\mathrm{disc}}\,D_i + W_{\mathrm{lat}}\,B_i,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{disc}}+W_{\mathrm{lat}}=1,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
which is independent of the Inf-TESLA++ mode-selection metric $C=R/L$ and leaves
mode selection untouched. The weighting is queue-dominant, because latency is
recoverable through receiver buffering whereas key loss is not. \emph{The
prototype evaluated in this paper uses the queue-dominant configuration
$W_{\mathrm{disc}}=1$, so $C_i=D_i$}; instrumenting $B_i$ as an independent
observable is left as future work, and we return to this choice in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Bounded Proportional Time-Scaling}
Rather than doubling or halving the slot---a bang-bang step that overshoots and
oscillates---we adjust the slot with a bounded proportional rule inspired by the
EIP-1559 fee mechanism~\cite{eip1559}. Let $C^{\ast}$ be the target utilization
and $\alpha$ a step gain. The next duration is
\begin{align}
\Delta_i &= C_i - C^{\ast}, \label{eq:delta}\\
T_{\mathrm{next}} &= \mathrm{clip}\!\Big(T_{\mathrm{cur}}\big(1+\alpha\,\Delta_i\big),\;
T_{\min},\;T_{\max}\Big).
\label{eq:update}
\end{align}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctrl} states the rule. With $C^{\ast}=0.5$ and $\alpha=0.25$, a
single step changes the slot by at most $\pm 12.5\%$ (attained at
$C_i\in\{0,1\}$), the slot grows above the target and shrinks below it, and the
unique interior fixed point is $C_i=C^{\ast}$, i.e.\ $Q_{\mathrm{len}}=
Q_{\mathrm{cap}}/2$. When the queue saturates ($C_i\gg 1$) the step is large and
$T_{\mathrm{next}}$ clamps to $T_{\max}$; the clip enforces the on-chain bounds
regardless of the raw computation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Bounded Proportional Time-Scaling (per slot boundary)}
\label{alg:ctrl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $D_i,\ B_i,\ T_{\mathrm{cur}},\ T_{\min},\ T_{\max},\
W_{\mathrm{disc}},\ W_{\mathrm{lat}},\ C^{\ast},\ \alpha$
\ENSURE $T_{\mathrm{next}}$
\STATE $C_i \gets W_{\mathrm{disc}}D_i + W_{\mathrm{lat}}B_i$
\STATE $\Delta_i \gets C_i - C^{\ast}$
\STATE $T_{\mathrm{next}} \gets T_{\mathrm{cur}}\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta_i)$
\STATE $T_{\mathrm{next}} \gets \max(T_{\min},\min(T_{\mathrm{next}},T_{\max}))$
\RETURN $T_{\mathrm{next}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Why the action relieves the queue.} The controller lengthens the slot
when the queue is full. This is self-relieving for two compounding reasons.
First, the sender produces exactly one disclosure object per slot, so production
runs at $1/T$ objects per second: a longer slot produces disclosures less often.
Second, a longer slot accumulates a larger batch, and the Bloom filter is MAC'd
and disclosed once per batch, so the per-message transmission overhead falls.
Both effects lower the byte rate the authentication path must sustain, giving the
radio time to drain the backlog; when the backlog clears, $C_i$ falls below
$C^{\ast}$ and the slot shrinks back toward $T_{\min}$, restoring low latency. The
disclosure queue is the correct signal precisely because the control action
reduces its arrival rate; a signal taken from the pre-transmission ingest buffer
would instead grow with $T$ and yield positive feedback.

\section{Security and Safety Analysis}

\subsection{Timing Safety Under Adaptive Slot Duration}
The primary security concern introduced by adaptive slot durations is
desynchronization. Unlike the fixed-slot baseline, receivers cannot assume a
globally known disclosure interval because the sender may legitimately vary the
slot duration within the configured range. Prob-Adaptive Inf-TESLA++ prevents the
resulting attack by treating the sender's runtime slot duration as untrusted:
receivers derive their verification delay solely from the immutable upper bound
$T_{\max}$ stored in the contract. Since every admissible slot satisfies
$T_{\mathrm{cur}}\le T_{\max}$, a receiver that waits according to this bound
cannot be induced to accept a disclosed key prematurely, regardless of any timing
information carried by adversarial packets. Here $d$ is a fixed, small
protocol constant (the disclosure delay in slots, $d=2$ in our evaluation) that
is part of the published policy, not a runtime value the receiver must infer.

\begin{theorem}[Timing Safety]
Let the adaptive slot duration satisfy $T_{\mathrm{cur}}\in[T_{\min},T_{\max}]$,
where $T_{\min}$ and $T_{\max}$ are stored immutably in the smart contract. Let
$d$ denote the disclosure delay (in slots) and $\delta_{\mathrm{net}}$ the
maximum one-way network delay. If every receiver waits
$\Delta \ge d\,T_{\max}+\delta_{\mathrm{net}}$ before accepting a disclosed key,
then the TESLA timing condition holds for all
$T_{\mathrm{cur}}\in[T_{\min},T_{\max}]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $T_{\min}\le T_{\mathrm{cur}}\le T_{\max}$, we have
$d\,T_{\mathrm{cur}}\le d\,T_{\max}$, so the maximum disclosure interval is bounded
by $d\,T_{\max}$. Waiting $\Delta \ge d\,T_{\max}+\delta_{\mathrm{net}}$ ensures
that key disclosure occurs only after the maximum disclosure interval and network
delay. Therefore an adversary cannot obtain $K_i$ early enough to forge packets
while slot-$i$ packets remain in transit, preserving the TESLA timing condition.
Since $T_{\max}$ is immutable and obtained from the contract, manipulated runtime
timing information cannot induce premature key acceptance.
\end{proof}

As a concrete illustration, with $d=2$ and $T_{\max}=8000$\,ms a receiver waits at
least $16$\,s (plus $\delta_{\mathrm{net}}$) before trusting a key, whatever slot
length the sender is currently using; a sender running at $T_{\mathrm{cur}}=1000$
ms simply discloses well inside this conservative bound, which costs latency but
never safety.

\subsection{Authentication Continuity Under Congestion}
As disclosure-queue occupancy approaches capacity ($D_i\!\rightarrow\!1$), the
controller progressively increases the slot duration up to $T_{\max}$, preserving
key continuity at the cost of higher authentication latency. Unlike fixed-slot
Inf-TESLA++, which may permanently lose disclosure keys after queue overflow, the
adaptive protocol trades additional latency for uninterrupted authentication,
until a hardware floor at which even $T_{\max}$ cannot balance production and
drainage (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Resistance to Resource Exhaustion and DoS}
During queue-flooding attacks, rising occupancy raises $C_i$ and enlarges the
slot before disclosure-queue overflow occurs. An attacker cannot push the slot
beyond the immutable bound $T_{\max}$, and once congestion subsides the slot
returns toward $T_{\min}$ under the proportional rule; because each step is a
bounded fraction of $T_{\mathrm{cur}}$, recovery is geometric in the contracted
dynamic range $T_{\max}/T_{\min}$.

\section{Implementation and Evaluation}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Implementation}
The extension is written in Go on top of the Inf-TESLA++ codebase. The sender
uses an \texttt{AdaptiveSlotSource} whose timer is re-armed with the duration
chosen by Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctrl}; a disclosure worker holds each produced
object for $d$ slots and then transmits it. The contract side adds
\texttt{storeAdaptiveKey} and a widened getter in Solidity with matching Go
bindings; all existing functions are untouched, so a non-adaptive deployment is
unchanged.

To characterize the controller against the resource whose overflow actually
destroys authentication, the implementation models a shared but budget-limited
radio. Authentication traffic (HMACs and key disclosures) is transmitted over a
throttled path limited to a configurable byte rate, while application data is
carried on an unthrottled plane; the throttle serializes authentication sends and
blocks each for its modelled transmission time. This isolates the disclosure
pipeline as the bottleneck the controller relieves and makes the radio budget the
single independent variable, with no dependence on operating-system traffic
shaping.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Every measurement runs the complete system: a local Ethereum node, the consortium
manager, the key owner (which registers commitments and pre-generates device
identities), and one RCD in the probabilistic, adaptive mode. The RCD broadcasts
over the loopback datagram channel and also receives and verifies its own
traffic, so a single node exercises both the sender-side controller and the
receiver-side checks.

We sweep the authentication-channel radio budget $B$ (bytes/second) over
$\{100,150,200,250,300,350,500,800\}$. For each budget the RCD runs for $90$\,s
at an application message rate of $50$\,msg/s, with $T_{\min}=1000$\,ms,
$T_{\max}=8000$\,ms, disclosure delay $d=2$, target $C^{\ast}=0.5$, gain
$\alpha=0.25$, capacity $Q_{\mathrm{cap}}=8$, and hash-chain length $512$. Each
budget is run in three independent iterations with a fresh device identity; we
report the median across iterations, with the min--max range as the error
interval. At each slot boundary the sender logs the current duration $T_i$ and the
backlog $Q_{\mathrm{len}}$; the receiver reports counts of verified messages and
of disclosures rejected by the security-condition check. From the per-slot trace
we extract the peak and a steady-state value (the median of the last $40\%$ of
slots, robust to the ramp and to the controller's limit cycle).

\subsection{Slot Duration Tracks the Bottleneck}
Figure~\ref{fig:slot} plots the steady-state and peak slot duration against the
radio budget, and Table~\ref{tab:results} lists the values. Three regimes are
visible. In the \emph{saturated} regime ($B\lesssim 150$\,B/s) the peak slot pins
at the $T_{\max}=8000$\,ms ceiling: even the longest admissible slot cannot make
the authentication path keep up. In the \emph{proportional} regime ($200\le B\le
300$\,B/s) the slot duration falls monotonically as the budget grows---the
controller sets a graded operating point, the direct evidence that the toggle
responds to load. In the \emph{idle} regime ($B\gtrsim 300$\,B/s) the slot rests
at the $T_{\min}=1000$\,ms floor, since the radio drains disclosures faster than
slots produce them. The steady-state curve dips slightly below the peak at the two
lowest budgets; this is a finite-run effect (at $8$\,s slots a $90$\,s run
contains few control ticks), and the peak curve confirms both points reach the
ceiling.

\subsection{The Backlog Stays Bounded}
Figure~\ref{fig:backlog} plots the disclosure backlog. For every budget at which
authentication is viable, the backlog remains a small multiple of the controller
target ($Q_{\mathrm{cap}}/2=4$) and well under the device buffer: the loop caps
the queue instead of letting it grow without bound. Only at the starved end
($B=100$\,B/s) does the backlog climb to $14$--$23$ objects, beyond
$Q_{\mathrm{cap}}$, because no slot length can balance production and drainage
there. That point marks the protocol's stability boundary.

\subsection{Authentication Survives to a Budget Floor}
Figure~\ref{fig:verif} reports receiver outcomes. Above the stability boundary,
verification is healthy (hundreds of messages per run at $500$--$800$\,B/s). As
the budget tightens through the transition the verified count declines but stays
positive, and at the starved $100$\,B/s point it collapses to zero: the
authentication path is so throttled that disclosures cannot reach the receiver
within its timing window, which is exactly the permanent-loss failure the fixed
timer cannot avoid. Premature rejections are negligible everywhere except a single
spike at $350$\,B/s; as explained in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} this is a timing
artifact of the prototype's slot counter at the $T_{\min}$ knee, not a forgery.

\subsection{The Control Loop, Over Time}
Figure~\ref{fig:step} shows a single run in the transition regime as a time
series. The backlog and the slot duration trace anti-phase waves: the backlog
rises above the target, the controller raises $T_i$ a few slots later, the longer
slot drains the backlog, $T_i$ then decays, and the cycle repeats. This is the
closed loop in action---the backlog is the cause and the slot duration is the
response, with the expected controller lag. The bounded proportional step, plus
the discrete jumps in Bloom-filter size, make the system limit-cycle around the
target rather than settle to a fixed point, which is why we report robust
steady-state statistics rather than a single terminal value.

\subsection{Computational and Communication Overhead}
The adaptive mechanism introduces negligible runtime overhead. At each slot
boundary the sender performs one weighted-sum evaluation, one subtraction, one
multiply, and a clamp---constant-time operations independent of the queue length
or receiver population. No additional broadcast packets, receiver messages, or
blockchain queries are introduced during normal operation. The only persistent
storage overhead is two integer timing parameters recorded once during session
initialization through \texttt{storeAdaptiveKey}. Table~\ref{tab:overhead}
summarizes the resulting online overhead relative to Inf-TESLA++.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The evaluation confirms the protocol's primary objective: the slot duration
tracks the authentication bottleneck, the disclosure backlog stays bounded across
the viable range, and authentication survives down to a hard budget floor at which
the queue overflows and, as the static protocol also would, verification fails.
We report the prototype honestly; several caveats bound the strength of the claims
and point at next steps.

\textbf{Single-factor congestion signal.} The evaluated controller is driven by
the queue-pressure term $D_i$ alone ($W_{\mathrm{disc}}=1$). The latency term
$B_i$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:score} is part of the design but is not yet
instrumented as an independent observable; adding it, and calibrating
$L_{\mathrm{base}}$ per device, is future work. Because $D_i$ is exactly the
signal whose overflow destroys authentication, the single-factor prototype is
sufficient to demonstrate the mechanism.

\textbf{Slot-counter timing.} In the current build the disclosure worker advances
the slot counter through a second timer instance in addition to the main slot
loop, so the counter progresses at roughly twice the intended rate and the
effective wall-clock disclosure hold is about half of $d$ slots. Near the
$T_{\min}$ knee this releases keys slightly early relative to the receiver's
security-condition threshold and produces the isolated premature-rejection spike
at $350$\,B/s. The fix---having the worker poll the shared slot counter rather
than opening its own timer---is mechanical and would tighten the transition band;
we report results with the artifact present and flag it as such.

\textbf{Finite-run steady state.} At the lowest budgets the slot is several
seconds long, so a $90$\,s run contains only a handful of control decisions and
the slot is still climbing toward the ceiling when the run ends. The steady-state
statistic there is therefore conservative; the peak statistic (which reaches
$T_{\max}$) better represents the operating point, and longer runs would close the
gap.

\textbf{Single node, and no fixed-slot baseline arm.} Sender and receiver are one
process over the loopback channel, which exercises the full controller and the
receiver checks but does not model independent clocks, multi-hop delay, or a
receiver population. The study also characterizes only the adaptive protocol; a
direct comparison against fixed-slow and fixed-fast arms over the same budget
sweep, and a secondary open-loop packet-loss axis, remain future work.

\textbf{Bloom-filter growth as a hard limit.} Because the batch, and hence the
Bloom filter, grows with the slot duration, the per-second authentication byte
rate has a floor set by the message rate. This floor is what creates the hard
stability boundary at the starved end; it is a genuine property of the protocol,
not of the measurement, and bounds what any slot policy can achieve.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
We presented Prob-Adaptive Inf-TESLA++, a backward-compatible extension of
Inf-TESLA++ that replaces the fixed slot timer with a closed-loop timer driven by
a local congestion signal and a bounded proportional update rule, clamped to
immutable on-chain bounds $[T_{\min},T_{\max}]$ that preserve the TESLA timing
condition and inherit the tamper-evidence and DoS resistance of the baseline. The
per-slot cost is constant arithmetic with no additional network messages. Running
the full stack---blockchain, owner, and RCD---and sweeping the
authentication-channel radio budget, we measured the controller tracking the
bottleneck: the slot rises to its ceiling as the budget starves, the disclosure
backlog stays bounded across the viable range, and authentication survives down to
a hard budget floor set by the protocol's own batching. The adaptive sender
therefore extends the range of radio conditions over which continuous
authentication is preserved, paying only additional latency when the channel is
genuinely scarce.

%==============================================================================
\section{Future Work}
Future work includes fixing the prototype's slot-counter timing and repeating the
budget sweep; adding a direct comparison against fixed-slow and fixed-fast
baselines and a secondary open-loop packet-loss axis; instrumenting the latency
term $B_i$ and calibrating it per device; extending the evaluation to an
independent multi-node testbed; and developing a receiver-enforced real-time bound
with a machine-checked timing-safety proof in TLA+ or Tamarin.
